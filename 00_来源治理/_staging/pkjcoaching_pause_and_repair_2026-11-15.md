# Overstimulated While Parenting? A Pause-and-Repair Plan for ADHD Families

By Pen King Jr. · Published October 6, 2026

**Quick answer:** Feeling overstimulated or "touched out" — too much noise, touch, mess and demand all at once — is a common part of parenting, and it can happen whether or not you have ADHD. It's also separate from your child's diagnosis: your overload is about your capacity in that moment, not proof that something is wrong with you or your child. A pause-and-repair plan has four parts: make sure your child is safe, cut some of the input, ask for help if you can, and repair afterwards if you shouted.

**Who this is for:** Written for parents who feel overstimulated or "touched out" — whether or not you have ADHD yourself — and especially for those parenting a child with ADHD. The steps are about the adult's overload, not the child's diagnosis.

## Two different things happening at once

When a house gets loud, it's easy to blur two things together: what's going on for your child, and what's going on for you. They affect each other, but they're not the same. Your child may have ADHD, a hard afternoon, or both. Separately, you may be at the end of what your senses and patience can take today — because of noise, being climbed on, a messy room, a long workday, poor sleep, or your own ADHD, anxiety or sensory sensitivity.

The Harley Therapy blog describes sensory overload in parents as something that can affect highly sensitive parents and those with ADHD, autism, anxiety or complex trauma, and describes being "touched out" as the feeling that "you would like to have your body for yourself." Naming which part is yours doesn't blame anyone. It just tells you where you can act right now.

## An example

Here is an illustrative example, not a real family. It's 5:40pm. One child is melting down about a screen ending, the other is singing at full volume, the pan is about to burn, and a toddler wants to be held. The parent notices their jaw is clenched and their voice is getting sharper. Instead of the next instruction, they turn the hob off, say "I'm getting overwhelmed — I'm taking one minute," turn off the TV, and stand in the doorway where they can still see everyone. It doesn't fix the evening. It does stop the next five minutes from getting worse.

## The pause-and-repair plan

1. **Check safety first.** Is anyone in danger? Is the stove on, the bath running, a young child somewhere they can't be left? Deal with that before anything else. The Child Mind Institute suggests stepping back or leaving the room for a minute "if it's safe to" — and notes that stepping away can't be done when a child is being aggressive or destructive. If you can't leave, stay, and go quiet instead.

2. **Cut some input.** Turn off the TV or music, lower the lights, move one step away from the noise, put the toddler down safely for a moment. Harley Therapy's suggestions include cutting background sound and switching off unneeded screens. Even one less source of noise can make a difference.

3. **Say what you're doing, out loud.** "I'm getting overwhelmed. I'm going to take a minute to calm down. I'm right here." The Child Mind Institute points out that telling your child you're taking a time-out models a way for them to self-soothe too.

4. **Take a breath, or count, or both.** Whatever is comfortable — there's no right technique for this moment.

5. **Ask for help if anyone is around.** A partner, a relative, a neighbour you trust. Harley Therapy suggests asking someone to take over so you can have at least 10 to 15 minutes to yourself, and says stepping away to regulate yourself "doesn't make you a bad parent." A text can be enough: "I'm at my limit. Can you take the kids for 15 minutes?"

## Repair after shouting

Most parents yell sometimes. What you do afterwards still matters. Once everyone is calm:

- **Own it, briefly.** The Child Mind Institute offers wording like "I'm sorry I lost my temper. I shouldn't yell at you."
- **Make it clear it wasn't their fault**, especially with younger children, and skip the "but" ("I'm sorry, but you weren't listening").
- **Reassure them.** "Even when I get upset, I still love you" — close to the line one father shares in the same article.
- **Keep the limit if there was one.** Repair doesn't mean the screen comes back on. It means you take responsibility for your part.
- **Name one change.** "Next time I'm getting loud, I'm going to step into the hallway for a minute."

## What this plan is — and isn't

It's a way to get through a hard minute and come back to your child afterwards. It isn't a promise that meltdowns stop, and a breathing exercise won't prevent every hard evening. It can help to notice your own early signs (a clenched jaw, a hot face, a sharper voice) and to plan for known hotspots like the after-school homecoming.

If you often feel at the edge, feel hopeless, or are worried you might hurt yourself or someone else, please talk to a doctor or therapist. If you're in crisis in the US, call or text 988.
